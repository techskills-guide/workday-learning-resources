Workday HCM Testing Guide: How to Build Test Cases for Hire, Transfer, Promotion and Termination Scenarios

A useful HCM test case does more than say “check the transaction.” It defines the business situation, starting conditions, steps, expected outcome and evidence needed to decide whether a result is correct.

Human Capital Management supports important workforce events, including hiring, job changes, promotions and employment exits. When learners explore these processes, it is easy to focus only on the happy path: enter information, submit the event and see whether it completes. A stronger approach is to design repeatable tests that explain what should happen, what could go wrong and how the result will be evaluated.

This guide uses fictional examples and general quality-assurance concepts. It is not official Workday documentation. Available actions, labels, business rules and routing vary by organization and tenant. Practise only in an authorized non-production environment with approved sample data.

1. Start with a business outcome, not a screen

Before writing steps, describe the outcome in plain language. For example: “A new worker is created with the approved start date, organization and job details, and the relevant next steps are available to the authorized roles.” This is more durable than a test that depends on a particular button label or screen layout.

Turn the outcome into questions:

  • What business event is being tested?
  • What conditions must be true before it begins?
  • Which fields or decisions matter to the business result?
  • What should be visible or completed at the end?
  • Which conditions would make the test fail?

Use the organization's approved requirements and process documentation as the source of expected behavior. Do not invent a rule simply because it seems reasonable.

2. Use a consistent test-case template

Every test should be easy for another learner or tester to repeat. A compact template keeps attention on the logic rather than lengthy notes.

FieldWhat to capture
Test ID and titleA unique reference and a short description of the scenario
ObjectiveThe business outcome the test is meant to confirm
PreconditionsApproved setup, fictional worker profile, role and required prior state
Test dataOnly the sample values needed to execute the scenario
StepsNumbered actions in the approved practice environment
Expected resultThe result supported by the documented business requirement
Actual resultWhat happened during this specific run
Status and evidencePass, fail or blocked, plus safe notes or approved screenshots

Keep expected and actual results separate. If they are written as one sentence, it becomes harder to tell whether the tester observed the outcome or merely assumed it.

3. Design lifecycle scenarios separately

Hiring, transfer, promotion and termination can involve different data, approvals, dates and downstream effects. Avoid putting every event into one oversized test case. Split scenarios so a failure can be traced to a clear business condition.

Scenario A: New hire

Objective: Verify that a fictional new-hire scenario follows the approved setup and records the intended start date and job-related details.

  • Precondition: use a fictional candidate/worker profile and a permitted practice environment.
  • Test focus: required information, valid organization and job selections, effective date and completion state.
  • Expected result: the event reflects the documented requirements and the next expected steps are available to the appropriate roles.

Scenario B: Transfer

Objective: Confirm that a fictional worker's transfer reflects the intended destination and effective date.

  • Precondition: the sample worker exists and the destination values are valid for the exercise.
  • Test focus: current versus proposed organization, effective date and any required review.
  • Expected result: the intended change is represented according to the approved process, without confusing future-dated information with current-state information.

Scenario C: Promotion

Objective: Check that a proposed promotion contains the intended job-related details and follows the relevant business rules.

  • Test focus: proposed position or job profile, effective date and any compensation-related data required by the test specification.
  • Expected result: each required value matches the approved scenario and the event reaches the documented end state.

Scenario D: Termination

Objective: Verify that a fictional termination scenario follows the approved rules for the event and its effective date.

  • Test focus: reason selection from approved sample values, effective date and expected follow-up tasks.
  • Expected result: the process reflects the documented outcome and relevant downstream responsibilities are handled according to policy.

These are test-design examples, not instructions to execute real workforce changes. Termination and other sensitive events must never be tested against real workers without explicit authorization and formal controls.

4. Include negative tests and boundary conditions

A positive test checks whether a valid scenario works. A negative test checks whether an invalid or incomplete scenario is handled as expected. Both are necessary, but negative testing should be based on documented rules rather than guesswork.

Test ideaQuestion to answerEvidence to record
Required detail omittedDoes the process identify the missing requirement clearly?Field or validation message and test ID
Invalid sample valueIs the value rejected or handled according to the documented rule?Input condition and observed response
Effective date boundaryDoes the result match the expected current or future state for the date used?Scenario date and expected timing
Unauthorized roleDoes access follow the approved role permissions?Approved test role and observed access outcome
Repeated submission attemptDoes the documented process prevent or explain duplicate handling?Steps taken and resulting state

Do not deliberately probe access boundaries or submit duplicate events in a live environment. Use only test conditions explicitly approved for the exercise.

5. Make expected results measurable

“The transaction should work” is too vague. A measurable expected result states what must be true at the end. Depending on the documented requirement, it may include a specific effective date, a selected organization, a completed process stage, a validation message or an expected downstream task.

For example, a fictional transfer test might specify: “When the approved test is complete, the proposed destination and effective date match the scenario specification, and the event reaches the documented completion state.” The actual result can then be compared with each condition individually.

Be careful with timing. A future-dated change may be recorded before it becomes effective. A test should distinguish between the event being entered, the event being approved and the intended effective state. Confirm the relevant behavior against the approved requirement rather than assuming every date should immediately change every view.

6. Track failures without guessing at the cause

A failed test means the observed result differs from the expected result; it does not automatically prove a product defect. The cause may be test data, a misunderstood requirement, setup, access, timing or configuration. Record evidence first and classify the cause only when there is enough information.

  1. Repeat the test using the same approved sample data and documented steps.
  2. Confirm the preconditions and compare the expected result with the actual result.
  3. Identify the exact step where the result first diverged.
  4. Record the environment, test ID, date and relevant non-sensitive evidence.
  5. Raise configuration or access concerns through the authorized team instead of changing settings independently.
  6. After an approved fix, rerun the failed case and any related regression tests.
Useful rule: Preserve the original failure evidence before changing test data or repeating steps. Otherwise, the cause may become harder to reproduce.

7. Build a small test matrix for practice

A test matrix helps show whether the main business scenarios and meaningful variations are covered. Start small and add cases based on requirements, risk and observed gaps.

8. Common mistakes to avoid

  • Testing several changes at once: if the result differs, it becomes difficult to identify which condition mattered.
  • Using real employee data casually: use approved fictional or masked data and follow data-handling policy.
  • Writing vague expected results: define the observable outcome before running the test.
  • Ignoring effective dates: record the date context and distinguish entry, approval and effective state.
  • Marking a blocked test as failed: if a precondition or environment is unavailable, record it as blocked and explain why.
  • Skipping regression: a change that fixes one case may affect a related scenario, so retest the agreed scope.

9. How HCM learners can practise this skill

Start with a written scenario and a blank test-case template. Define the expected result using a business requirement, then write one positive test and one approved negative test. Ask another learner to follow the steps without extra explanation. If they cannot reproduce the expected outcome, improve the instructions or clarify the requirement.

Frequently asked questions

What is an HCM test case?

It is a repeatable set of preconditions, sample data, steps and expected results used to check whether a defined business scenario behaves as required.

Should beginners test only successful scenarios?

No. A useful learning plan includes valid scenarios and approved negative cases, such as missing required information or a date boundary. The expected behavior must come from the documented requirement.

What is the difference between a failed and blocked test?

A failed test ran and produced a result that did not meet the expected outcome. A blocked test could not be completed because a prerequisite, environment or authorization was unavailable.

Can I practise with real worker records?

Use fictional or formally approved test data. Real employee information should only be accessed or used for testing when explicit authorization and organizational controls allow it.

Does one successful test prove the whole process works?

No. It proves only that the specific case passed in the tested conditions. Coverage depends on the business rules, variations, risk and agreed regression scope.
