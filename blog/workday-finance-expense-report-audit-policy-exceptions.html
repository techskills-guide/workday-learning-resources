Workday Finance Expense Report Audits: Reviewing Policy Exceptions Before Reimbursement

A practical guide to checking expense details, supporting documents, coding and review decisions before an expense report moves forward for payment.

Expense reports can look complete at first glance and still contain details that need attention. A receipt may not support the amount entered, an expense category may not match the business purpose, or a transaction may fall outside an organisation’s travel and reimbursement policy. These issues are not always signs of misuse. Sometimes they are simple data-entry mistakes, missing explanations or differences in how a policy is interpreted.

In Workday Financial Management, expense report workflows can be configured to support review, approval, audit checks and correction. For finance learners, the important skill is understanding what to validate, why an exception matters and how to document the next action rather than treating every flagged line as an automatic rejection.

What an expense report audit is designed to check

An expense report review is a structured examination of the information submitted for reimbursement. Depending on an organisation’s configuration and policy, reviewers may check:

  • Business purpose: whether the description explains why the expense was incurred and how it relates to work.
  • Receipt evidence: whether the attached receipt or other permitted support matches the date, merchant, currency and amount entered.
  • Expense classification: whether the selected expense item accurately describes the purchase.
  • Policy limits: whether the claim follows applicable limits, eligibility rules and documentation requirements.
  • Duplicate or unusual entries: whether the same purchase appears more than once or an amount looks inconsistent with the supporting details.
  • Financial coding: whether relevant worktags or other accounting classifications direct the cost to the appropriate business area.

The exact checks vary by company, country, expense type and tenant configuration. A reviewer should use the organisation’s current policy and configured process as the source of truth.

How an exception differs from an error

An exception is a detail that needs further review; it is not necessarily proof that the claim is invalid. For example, a hotel bill may exceed a normal nightly guideline because the employee attended an event in a high-demand location. The reviewer may need an explanation or additional approval rather than an outright rejection.

An error is a confirmed inaccuracy that must be corrected before the report can proceed. Examples include entering the receipt total incorrectly, selecting the wrong expense item or attaching a document that belongs to another transaction.

Practical distinction: An exception asks, “Does this need an explanation or additional review?” An error asks, “Which information is incorrect and how should it be corrected?”

A practical review workflow in Workday

1. Start with the report-level details

Review the employee or payee, report period, currency, total amount and stated business purpose. Confirm that the report is being reviewed under the correct policy and that the supporting context is understandable before examining individual lines.

2. Compare each expense line with its evidence

Check the date, merchant, amount and currency against the receipt or permitted supporting record. For itemised bills, confirm that the claimed components are clear. If evidence is missing, follow the organisation’s missing-receipt procedure rather than assuming that a blank attachment automatically invalidates the expense.

3. Validate the expense item and coding

Ask whether the selected expense item represents the actual purchase. Then review relevant financial classifications, such as cost centre, project or other worktags used by the organisation. Incorrect coding can affect downstream budget analysis and reporting even when the reimbursement amount itself is correct.

4. Investigate policy exceptions and unusual patterns

Look for claims above a policy threshold, duplicate-looking entries, unusual amounts or expense types that require additional review. Compare the details with the available business context. Where the reason is unclear, request clarification or route the item to the appropriate reviewer.

5. Record the decision and follow the configured process

Depending on the assigned role and business process configuration, a reviewer may be able to edit permitted details, approve, deny or send the report back. Some configurations support sending individual lines back while allowing other lines to continue. Use the action that matches the issue and the organisation’s approval rules; do not bypass required controls.

Worked example: reviewing a travel expense report

Imagine an employee submits a travel report containing three lines:

  • Hotel: ₹8,400, with a receipt attached.
  • Taxi: ₹1,250, with a digital receipt.
  • Client meal: ₹3,600, with a receipt but no attendee details in the description.

The reviewer confirms that the hotel and taxi amounts match their supporting documents and that the expense items and coding are appropriate. The meal receipt supports the amount, but the business context is incomplete. Rather than rejecting the entire report, the reviewer checks the applicable meal policy and requests the missing attendee or business-purpose details if required. The report can then proceed according to the configured workflow once the issue is resolved.

This example shows why line-level review matters: one incomplete item does not automatically mean every other expense line is incorrect.

Common mistakes made during expense reviews

  • Approving a report based only on its total instead of validating individual lines.
  • Assuming every policy exception is misconduct instead of checking the business context.
  • Ignoring expense coding because the receipt amount is correct.
  • Sending an entire report back when the configured process allows a specific line to be corrected.
  • Making undocumented changes that leave the next reviewer without a clear explanation.
  • Using a generic checklist without checking the organisation’s current policy and workflow.

What finance learners should practise

A useful practice exercise is to create a fictional expense report with a mixture of clean lines and review issues. Use sample data only—never real employee details, payment information or confidential company receipts.

  1. Prepare five fictional expense lines with different categories and amounts.
  2. Introduce two controlled issues, such as a mismatched receipt amount and an incomplete business purpose.
  3. Identify which details are errors and which are exceptions requiring clarification.
  4. Review the expense item and financial coding for each line.
  5. Write a short reviewer note explaining the decision and the next action.

This exercise develops the judgement needed to work with expense processes: validating evidence, understanding policy, identifying downstream accounting implications and communicating corrections clearly.

How expense report reviews connect with wider Workday Finance knowledge

Expense management is connected to more than reimbursement. Expense classifications and accounting information can influence financial reporting, budget visibility and audit evidence. Understanding the review process therefore helps learners see how operational transactions become part of an organisation’s wider financial controls.

When studying this area, connect expense report concepts with business process steps, approval routing, accounting classifications, reporting and reconciliation. The goal is not to memorise a single screen sequence; it is to understand why each validation exists and how configuration can differ between organisations.
