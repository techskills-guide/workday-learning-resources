Workday Finance Customer Payment Application: How to Investigate Unapplied Cash

A practical guide to matching customer receipts with open invoices, reviewing payment differences and understanding unapplied cash.

Receiving a customer payment does not always mean that the related invoice is fully settled. A business may receive money without enough remittance information to identify the correct invoice, or the amount received may differ from the outstanding balance. Until the receipt is matched and applied correctly, finance teams may see an unapplied or partially applied cash balance.

In Workday Financial Management, customer payment application is part of the broader accounts receivable process. The exact tasks and available options depend on the organisation’s configuration, security roles and payment channels. The underlying investigation, however, follows a consistent principle: identify the receipt, establish which customer obligation it relates to, and resolve any difference with an appropriate audit trail.

What does customer payment application mean?

Customer payment application is the process of associating a received payment with one or more open customer invoices or receivable items. When the payment is correctly applied, the related outstanding balance is reduced or cleared according to the amount and accounting rules.

For example, if a customer pays ₹50,000 against an invoice for ₹50,000, the receipt may be applied to settle that open item. If the customer sends ₹45,000, the remaining ₹5,000 needs to be understood and handled according to the organisation’s process rather than being silently written off.

Why does cash remain unapplied?

Unapplied cash can occur for several reasons. Common situations include:

  • Missing remittance advice: the payment arrives without details identifying the invoice or invoices being paid.
  • Incorrect invoice reference: the reference supplied by the customer does not match the receivable record.
  • Multiple open invoices: the customer has several outstanding items and the intended allocation is unclear.
  • Short payment: the amount received is lower than the invoice balance.
  • Overpayment: the receipt exceeds the amount currently due.
  • Customer identification issue: the payment record does not clearly identify the correct customer account.
  • Timing difference: the receipt has arrived but the relevant invoice or supporting information is not yet available for matching.

Unapplied does not automatically mean that cash is missing. It means that the receipt has not yet been fully associated with the intended receivable item.

A structured process for investigating an unapplied receipt

1. Confirm the receipt details

Start with the available receipt information: amount, currency, receipt date, payment reference, payer details and payment channel. Check that the receipt has been recorded once and that the source information is complete enough to support further investigation.

2. Identify the customer account

Use the available remittance information and customer records to identify the likely payer. Be careful with similar customer names, parent and subsidiary relationships, or payments made by a third party on behalf of a customer. Follow the organisation’s controls before assigning a receipt to an account.

3. Review open receivable items

Examine the customer’s outstanding invoices and other relevant receivable items. Compare invoice references, dates, currencies and balances with the payment information. If the remittance advice lists several invoices, check whether the total allocation agrees with the amount received.

4. Investigate amount differences

If the receipt does not match an invoice balance, determine whether the difference is caused by a short payment, overpayment, deduction, bank charge, credit memo or another documented reason. Do not assume that a difference should be written off. The appropriate treatment depends on company policy and the supporting evidence.

5. Apply or route the receipt using the configured process

Once the customer and intended receivable items are sufficiently supported, follow the organisation’s authorised payment application process. If the information remains unclear, keep the receipt in the appropriate unapplied or review status and request clarification from the customer or responsible internal team.

6. Confirm the resulting balance and retain evidence

After application, review the affected invoice balances and the customer account position. Retain remittance advice, correspondence and review notes as required. The investigation should make it possible for another finance colleague to understand why the receipt was applied in a particular way.

Worked example: one receipt, two open invoices

A customer has two open invoices: Invoice A for ₹32,000 and Invoice B for ₹18,000. The organisation receives a payment of ₹50,000, but the bank reference contains only the customer’s short name and no invoice numbers.

ItemAmount
Invoice A₹32,000
Invoice B₹18,000
Total open balance₹50,000
Receipt received₹50,000

The amounts appear to match, but the finance team should still confirm that the payment belongs to this customer and that both invoices are intended to be settled. If remittance advice or another reliable source confirms the allocation, the receipt can be applied according to the configured process. If the customer intended to pay a different invoice, applying it based only on matching totals could create an incorrect account balance.

How to handle short payments and overpayments

A short payment occurs when the amount received is less than the amount due. The customer may have deducted an agreed credit, disputed part of the invoice, made a calculation error or simply paid an incorrect amount. Review the remittance details and supporting documents before deciding how the remaining balance should be treated.

An overpayment occurs when the receipt exceeds the open amount being settled. Depending on policy, the excess may remain as an unapplied customer credit, be allocated to another open item or be handled through an approved refund process. Do not assume that an overpayment should automatically be refunded or applied to the oldest invoice.

Control point: Payment application should be supported by evidence. A matching amount is useful, but it is not by itself proof that a receipt belongs to a particular invoice.

Common mistakes during payment application

  • Applying a receipt to an invoice based only on a similar amount.
  • Ignoring customer account differences or third-party payer details.
  • Clearing short payments without documenting the reason.
  • Allocating an overpayment without checking the customer’s instructions and company policy.
  • Leaving no explanation for why a receipt was matched to particular invoices.
  • Failing to review the customer’s remaining open balance after application.

What Workday Finance learners should practise

A useful learning exercise is to create fictional customer accounts with several open invoices and a set of incoming receipts. Include one receipt with a clear invoice reference, one with missing remittance details, one short payment and one overpayment. For each case, record:

  1. Which customer account is supported by the available evidence.
  2. Which open receivable items may be relevant.
  3. Whether the receipt can be applied or needs clarification.
  4. What evidence should be retained for the decision.
  5. How the remaining customer balance should be checked afterward.

This exercise connects accounts receivable concepts with transaction review, customer account management, reconciliation and financial controls. Use fictional data only; never practise with real customer banking details or confidential account records.

Final thoughts

Unapplied cash is often a matching and information problem rather than a missing-payment problem. A disciplined review of receipt details, customer identity, open invoices and amount differences helps finance teams apply cash accurately and preserve a clear audit trail. For Workday Finance learners, understanding this process adds an important accounts receivable perspective alongside supplier payments, invoice processing and reconciliation.
